storybook channel, produced with Higgsfield's Faceless Studio (Fairy Tale & Myth type

## 5. Scripts, episodes 1 to 3 (paste straight into Higgsfield)

Each is 2 minutes at the storybook pace: around 330 to 380 words, one idea per
sentence, cold open on the twist, last line is the meaning. Blocks are 10 seconds.

### Episode 1: The king who got stuck between heaven and earth

Somewhere above you, right now, a king is hanging upside down. He has been there for
a very long time. This is how he got there.

His name was Satyavrata, and he ruled the kingdom of Ayodhya long before Rama was
born. He was a good king. He had one strange wish. He wanted to go to heaven without
dying first. In his own body. Alive.

He asked his family priest, the sage Vasishtha. The sage said no. Nobody goes up alive.
So the king asked the sage's sons. They did not just say no. They cursed him. His skin
turned dark, his jewels fell away, and he became an outcast. The people gave him a new
name. Trishanku. The one with three sins.

Trishanku had one door left. The sage Vishwamitra, who hated Vasishtha more than he
loved the rules. Vishwamitra agreed to perform the ritual. He called the gods to come
and lift the king. The gods did not come.

So Vishwamitra did it himself. He gathered all the power of his years of penance and
pushed. Trishanku rose. Past the trees. Past the clouds. Up to the gate of heaven.

Indra, the king of the gods, saw an outcast arriving alive. He threw him back down.
Headfirst.

Trishanku fell screaming. And Vishwamitra, furious, shouted one word. Stop.

The king stopped. Mid-air. Upside down.

Then Vishwamitra did something no sage had done. He began to build a new heaven. New
stars. New planets. A new Indra, for a new sky, with Trishanku as its first citizen.

Now the gods panicked. They came to bargain. Keep your stars, they said. Let them shine
in the southern sky forever. But stop building. And the king stays where he is.

Vishwamitra agreed.

So Trishanku hangs there still, among the stars that were made for him, not in heaven,
not on earth. Which is why, in India, when you are stuck between two choices and
cannot have either, people say you are in Trishanku's heaven.

He wanted to go up without letting go. That is the one thing the sky does not allow.

### Episode 2: The king who climbed onto the scale

A king once cut flesh from his own thigh to feed a bird. Then he cut more. Then he
stopped cutting, because he understood the trick.

His name was Shibi, and his kingdom was famous for one thing. No one who asked for
shelter was ever turned away.

One afternoon a dove crashed into his lap, bleeding, shaking. Behind it came a hawk.
The hawk landed on the arm of the throne and spoke. That dove is my food. Give it to
me, I am starving.

The king said no. The dove asked for shelter. Shelter is given.

The hawk said, then you are starving me instead. Is my life worth less than hers?

Shibi thought. Then he asked for a scale. He put the dove on one side. On the other,
he placed a piece of his own flesh, cut from his thigh, equal to the dove's weight.

The scale did not move. The dove was heavier.

He cut more. The dove was still heavier. He cut from his arms. From his chest. His
ministers wept. The scale would not balance. The small grey bird outweighed everything
a king could give.

And then Shibi understood. The bird was not asking for pieces.

He put down the knife, stepped onto the scale, and sat beside the dove with his whole
body.

The scale balanced.

The hawk became Indra, king of the gods. The dove became Agni, the god of fire. They
had come to test one thing. Not whether a king would give. Whether he would give
everything.

They healed his wounds and left.

The story is older than the Mahabharata, where it is told, and it is told again in
Buddhist Jataka tales, and in a hundred village versions since. It survives because
of one line in it.

A promise you keep in pieces is not kept. You keep it with the whole of you, or you
do not keep it at all.

### Episode 3: The demon who could burn anyone by touch

He had one power. Anyone he touched on the head turned to ash. Within an hour he had
used it on himself. This is how.

Bhasmasura was a demon who wanted to be unbeatable. So he did the one thing that
always works in these stories. He prayed. For years. He stood on one leg, he went
without food, he chanted Shiva's name until the mountain itself was tired of him.

Shiva appeared. Ask.

Bhasmasura had rehearsed this. Give me a touch, he said, that turns any head to ash.

Shiva gave it.

And Bhasmasura looked at Shiva and smiled. Let me test it, he said. On you.

The god of destruction ran.

Through forests, across rivers, down through the three worlds, Shiva ran, and the
demon ran behind him with his right hand raised. The gods watched from above and
did nothing, because what do you do when the destroyer is the one being hunted?

Vishnu did something.

On the road in front of Bhasmasura appeared a woman. Mohini. The most beautiful
person anyone had ever seen. The demon stopped running.

She asked him to dance with her. Just copy my steps, she said. Whatever I do, you do.

He copied her. Hand left. Hand right. A turn. A step. She lifted her hand to her hip.
He lifted his. She lifted her hand to her shoulder. He lifted his.

She placed her palm, gently, on the top of her own head.

And Bhasmasura, smiling, placed his palm on his.

There was a small pile of ash where a demon had been.

Shiva walked back. Vishnu let the disguise fall. And the story ends, as these stories
do, with the gods laughing about it later.

But look at what actually beat him. Not a weapon. Not a bigger god. A mirror. He was
handed everything he asked for, and the only thing in the world that could destroy
him was his own hand, doing what someone else did first.

Most of us are never beaten by an enemy. We are beaten by copying.

## 7. Scripts 4 and 5, outlines

**Episode 4, Why Yudhishthira lied only once.** Cold open: the man who had never lied
says four words and his teacher drops his weapons. The teacher is Drona. The lie is
"Ashwatthama is dead", true of an elephant, false of his son, with the second half said
under a blast of conches. Drona stops fighting and is killed. Yudhishthira's chariot,
which had always floated a hand above the ground, touches the earth for the first
time. Meaning: one half-truth cost him the only thing that set him apart.

**Episode 5, The boy who asked Death a question.** Cold open: a boy sits for three days
at Death's door and Death apologises to him. Nachiketa is given to Yama by his angry
father. Yama grants three wishes. The third: what happens after a person dies. Yama
offers gold, horses, long life, anything but that answer. The boy refuses it all.
Meaning: the one who turns down everything is the only one who gets the real answer.
