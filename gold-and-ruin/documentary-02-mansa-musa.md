# Gold and Ruin, Documentary 2
## The Richest Man Who Ever Lived, and the Road His Gold Travelled Without Him

Target: 10-11 minutes at a measured documentary pace (about 1,500 words).
Format: Higgsfield History long-form, Watercolor Chronicle, one narrator, subtitles on.
Structure: cold open, rewind, four chapters, close.
[SCENE] lines are visual cues for the generator, not narration.

Research date: 11 October 2026. Every figure below was checked against at least two
sources; where sources disagree, the script uses the cautious wording and the notes say why.

---

### COLD OPEN

[SCENE: Cairo, July 1324, dust-light over the citadel, a caravan filling the road from the south, the Nile glinting beyond]

In the summer of 1324, a king arrived outside Cairo with more gold than the city could
absorb.

He had crossed the Sahara. He was on his way to Mecca. And at every stop he gave gold
away. To officials. To mosques. To the poor. To anyone who sold him anything.

[SCENE: a Cairo money-changer's bench, a balance scale, gold dust tipping one pan, silver coins rising on the other]

A clerk in the Egyptian administration later wrote down what happened next. The price of
gold in Egypt fell. By his account, it stayed down for years.

[SCENE: title card over a watercolor of the Sahara at dusk, caravan tracks stretching to the horizon]

Headlines today call him the richest man who ever lived. Within three centuries of his
pilgrimage, his empire was gone, its cities had been sacked, and its gold was being carried
north by a foreign army. This is the story of Mansa Musa of Mali. This is Gold and Ruin.

### REWIND: WHERE THE GOLD CAME FROM

[SCENE: a river valley in West Africa, men panning in shallow pits, a nugget in a dark hand]

Start with the ground. In the upper valleys of the Senegal and Niger rivers lay two
goldfields, Bambuk and Bure. By some estimates, as much as two-thirds of the gold circulating in the medieval
Mediterranean came from West Africa.

[SCENE: a watercolor map, the Niger bending, Niani, trade routes running north into the desert]

The empire of Mali rose on top of that ground. Its founder, Sundiata Keita, won his
decisive battle around 1235. His successors ruled from a capital called Niani. They did not
dig the gold themselves. The miners kept their pits secret, even from their own kings. The
empire taxed what passed through.

[SCENE: Taghaza, a village built of salt blocks under camel-skin roofs, slabs being hacked from the ground]

And what passed through was a trade in two commodities. The goldfields had gold. They had
no salt. Twenty-five days' march into the desert, at a place called Taghaza, there was
almost nothing but salt. The houses were built of it. Enslaved miners cut it into slabs,
two to a camel, and the caravans carried it south.

The traveller Ibn Battuta saw the prices. A load of salt that fetched eight to ten gold
mithqals at the desert's edge sold for twenty, thirty, sometimes forty in Mali. Every mile
added value. And the king of Mali took his share of every mile.

[SCENE: a young Musa in a court, a vacant throne, a fleet of small ships on a dark Atlantic]

Around 1312 that king became a man named Musa. He told the Egyptians later how he had come
to the throne. His predecessor, he said, had sailed west into the Atlantic with two
thousand ships to find its far shore, and never came back. Some historians suspect a tidier
story: that Musa pushed him aside. Either way, by the 1320s Musa ruled the gold.

### CHAPTER ONE: THE CARAVAN

[SCENE: Niani at dawn, a vast caravan forming, camels being loaded, heralds with gold staffs catching the sun]

In 1324 he set out for Mecca. Every Muslim who is able is meant to make the pilgrimage
once. Musa decided to make it in a way nobody could forget.

The numbers come from Arab chroniclers writing years later, and they should be handled with
care. The usual figure is a caravan of around sixty thousand people, including some twelve
thousand enslaved attendants. Heralds walked ahead carrying staffs of gold. And then came
the camels.

[SCENE: a line of camels, each with leather sacks, one sack splitting to spill dust]

Most accounts say eighty to a hundred camels carried gold dust. How much each one carried
is where the sources fall apart. Estimates for the total run from a single tonne to nearly
twenty. Even the low end was a fortune.

[SCENE: the caravan crossing dunes, then the long swing east toward the Nile]

By July 1324, after months in the desert, the caravan was camped outside Cairo.

### CHAPTER TWO: THE DAY GOLD GOT CHEAPER

[SCENE: the Cairo citadel, a throne room, Musa standing, attendants gesturing at the floor]

The sultan of Egypt, al-Nasir Muhammad, was the most powerful ruler in the Islamic world.
Musa was told to kiss the ground before him. He refused. He said he would bow to God alone.
Then he did bow, carefully, and the sultan rose to greet him and seated him at his side.

The account comes from al-Umari, an official in Cairo who wrote it down some years later
from the memories of men who had been there. He was not a neutral witness. But he is the
best witness we have.

[SCENE: gold being carried into offices, a treasurer's face, coins stacked]

What he describes is a king who could not stop giving. Every official who dealt with him
received gold. The sultan received gold. The markets received gold, because Musa's people
bought everything they saw and paid whatever was asked.

[SCENE: the money-changer's scale from the cold open, the silver side now heavy]

And then the market did what markets do. Al-Umari says that before the Malians came, the
gold mithqal had never traded below twenty-five silver dirhams in Egypt. After them it fell,
and sat at twenty-two or less. By his reckoning, the slump lasted about twelve years.

That is the number behind every headline about Musa crashing the gold market. A fall of
roughly a tenth, by one official's memory, in one city. The figures of twenty or
twenty-five percent that appear in popular accounts are not in the source.

[SCENE: the caravan leaving Cairo for Mecca, thinner now, the gold sacks visibly fewer]

Musa went on to Mecca, spent more, and turned for home. By the time he reached Cairo
again, several accounts say, he had to borrow from the city's merchants to get back. The
richest man in the world went home in debt.

### CHAPTER THREE: THE MAP

[SCENE: Timbuktu, mud-brick walls rising, a mosque under construction, a robed figure from Andalusia directing workers]

He did not go home empty-handed. He brought back scholars, and an Andalusian poet named
Abu Ishaq al-Sahili, who is traditionally credited with building for him. Ibn Khaldun is
said to have recorded the fee: twelve thousand mithqals of gold dust. The great
Djinguereber mosque at Timbuktu dates from around 1327.

[SCENE: the Catalan Atlas, parchment, a black king on a gold throne holding a gold disc, a veiled rider on a camel approaching]

In Europe, the news arrived in the form of a map. In 1375, on the island of Majorca, a
Jewish cartographer named Abraham Cresques produced the Catalan Atlas. At the southern edge
of the Sahara sits a king in a gold crown, on a gold throne, holding up a lump of gold. The
caption names him as Musa of Mali, lord of Guinea, and calls him the richest and noblest
ruler in the whole region because of the gold that comes from his land.

It is the earliest surviving picture of him. It is also an advertisement. For two hundred
years, Europeans would look at that map and wonder how to reach the gold without the desert
in the way.

[SCENE: a modern phone screen scrolling a "richest people in history" list, then fading to parchment]

Today the internet puts a figure on Musa's wealth: four hundred billion dollars. It comes
from a 2012 ranking on a celebrity net worth website. It is not a calculation. Mali had no
balance sheet, no share price, and no currency that converts into ours. Historians say,
bluntly, that no number is possible. What the sources support is simpler. For a few
decades, one man collected the toll on the largest gold supply the Old World had.

### CHAPTER FOUR: WHAT HAPPENED TO THE GOLD

[SCENE: an empty throne, two dates on parchment, 1332 and 1337, a hand hesitating between them]

Musa died sometime between 1332 and 1337. The sources cannot agree on the year. His son
Maghan took the throne and, by most accounts, spent heavily and reigned briefly. Musa's
brother Sulayman followed and steadied things. When Ibn Battuta visited in 1352, the court
still impressed him. The king, he complained, was a miser.

[SCENE: a gold nugget the size of a boulder, hands loading it onto a cart, merchants counting coins]

Then came the decline. Sulayman died around 1360 and civil war followed. Ibn Khaldun wrote
that a later king, Mari Jata the Second, squandered the royal treasury, and sold off a gold
nugget so large it had been part of the kingdom's regalia. The gold, in other words, was
being sold cheap by men who had not earned it.

[SCENE: Tuareg riders entering Timbuktu; then Songhai war canoes on the Niger]

In the 1430s the Tuareg took Timbuktu. In 1468 the Songhai ruler Sunni Ali took it from
them, and Gao, once an eastern province of Mali, became the capital of a new empire. The
gold trade carried on. It simply paid a different king.

The Songhai kings knew the playbook. In 1496 Askia Muhammad made his own pilgrimage,
reportedly with three hundred thousand pieces of gold, a third of it given away in the holy
cities. Timbuktu grew richer than ever. A visitor in the early 1500s wrote that more profit
was made there from books than from any other trade.

[SCENE: Marrakesh, a sultan's court, a letter demanding the taxes of the Taghaza salt mines]

And then someone north of the desert decided to stop trading for the gold and simply take
it.

Ahmad al-Mansur, sultan of Morocco, began by demanding the tax revenue from the Taghaza
salt mines. The Songhai sent him a gift of gold instead. He demanded more. In 1590 he sent
an army south under a commander named Judar Pasha: a few thousand men, with firearms and
cannon. On the way they sacked Taghaza.

[SCENE: 13 March 1591, Tondibi, a cattle stampede, musket smoke, Songhai cavalry breaking]

On 13 March 1591, at Tondibi, north of Gao, the Songhai met them with a far larger army.
Askia Ishaq drove a herd of cattle at the Moroccan line. The muskets fired, the cattle
turned, and the empire that had inherited Musa's gold broke in an afternoon.

[SCENE: caravans leaving Timbuktu for the north, camels loaded; a scholar led away under guard]

Gao, Timbuktu and Djenne were stripped. Caravan after caravan of gold dust went north to
Marrakesh. Around 1594 the city's most famous scholar, Ahmad Baba, was taken across the
desert to Morocco, and his library went with him.

### CLOSE

[SCENE: Taghaza abandoned, salt ruins half-buried; then the salt pits of Taoudenni, still worked today]

Taghaza itself was abandoned. The salt trade moved to a new mine closer to Timbuktu. The
Moroccans held the river cities for decades, but they never held the goldfields. Those
stayed where they had always been, in the hands of miners who told no one where the pits
were.

[SCENE: the Catalan Atlas again, the gold disc in Musa's hand, the camera pulling back until the map is small]

Here is the thing to remember about Mansa Musa. His empire did not make gold. It stood
between the gold and the people who wanted it, and charged a toll. For a century, that toll
made it the richest state in Africa. When the empire weakened, the gold did not stop
flowing. It flowed to whoever held the road next. The Tuareg. The Songhai. The Moroccans.

The gold was never his. He held the gate for a while. And in 1324 he opened it wide enough
for the whole world to see what was behind it.

[SCENE: title card: GOLD AND RUIN]

Next time on Gold and Ruin: the mountain of silver that paid for the most powerful empire in
Europe, and the king who went bankrupt four times while he owned it.
