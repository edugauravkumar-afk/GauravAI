# Gold and Ruin, Documentary 1
## The Company That Conquered a Country, Then Asked for a Bailout

Target: 10-11 minutes at a measured documentary pace (about 1,500 words).
Format: Higgsfield History long-form, Watercolor Chronicle, one narrator, subtitles on.
Structure: cold open, rewind, four chapters, close.
[SCENE] lines are visual cues for the generator, not narration.

Research date: 10 October 2026. Every figure below was checked against at least two
sources; where sources disagree, the script uses the cautious wording and the notes say why.

---

### COLD OPEN

[SCENE: a dim London boardroom, candlelight, a long table of men in wigs, a single ledger open, rain on tall windows]

In the summer of 1773, a company went to the British government and asked for a rescue.
It had run out of money. Its shares had lost more than a third of their value in a year.
Its warehouses were full of tea nobody was buying.

[SCENE: the same ledger, a quill drawing a line under a column of numbers that end in red]

Governments bail out companies all the time. But this one was different. This company had
an army. It collected taxes from twenty million people. And it had just presided over a
famine that killed, by the most careful estimates, a third of a province.

[SCENE: title card over a watercolor map of India, trade routes glowing]

This is the story of how a group of merchants in a rented house in London came to rule the
richest region on earth. And how, at the very moment of their greatest power, they nearly
went bankrupt. This is Gold and Ruin.

### REWIND: 1600

[SCENE: Elizabethan London, the Thames crowded with ships, a parchment with a wax seal]

On the last day of the year 1600, Queen Elizabeth the First signed a charter. It gave a group
of a little over two hundred London merchants the exclusive right to trade with everything
east of Africa. Fifteen years. No competition.

They had pooled roughly seventy thousand pounds. By the standards of a kingdom, that was
pocket money. The Dutch had raised ten times as much for their own company two years later.

[SCENE: a small wooden ship leaving an English harbour, sailors hauling rope]

Nobody signing that paper imagined a conquest. They wanted pepper. They wanted cloth. They
wanted to buy cheap in the east and sell dear in London, and they wanted the Queen to stop
anyone else from doing the same.

For a century and a half, that is mostly what they did.

[SCENE: a Mughal court, marble, silk, a European envoy bowing low before an emperor on a raised throne]

Because in India, the Company was small. The Mughal emperors ruled a state with a
population many times that of all of Europe. The English were tolerated guests, trading from
a few fortified warehouses on the coast. They bowed. They paid. They asked permission.

Then the empire began to break.

### CHAPTER ONE: THE BATTLE THAT WAS NOT A BATTLE

[SCENE: 1757, a mango grove near a river in Bengal, two armies at dawn, monsoon clouds]

By the 1750s the Mughal centre had weakened and the provinces were going their own way.
Bengal, the richest of them, was ruled by a young and nervous nawab, Siraj ud-Daulah.

In June 1757, near a village called Plassey, he faced a Company force led by a thirty-one
year old clerk turned soldier named Robert Clive.

[SCENE: close on a sodden cannon, rain pouring, gunpowder spoiling]

On paper it was absurd. Clive had about three thousand men. The nawab had something like
fifty thousand, with war elephants and French-trained artillery.

But most of the nawab's army never fought. His senior general, Mir Jafar, had already made a
deal with the Company. When the fighting started, he held his troops back and watched.

[SCENE: Mir Jafar on horseback, still, as the battle moves without him]

Plassey was less a battle than a transaction. The nawab fled and was killed. Mir Jafar was
placed on the throne. And the Company presented its bill.

The new nawab paid the Company and its officers a fortune. Clive personally received a sum
that most accounts put at around two hundred and thirty-four thousand pounds. In today's
money, tens of millions. He was thirty-two.

[SCENE: chests of silver being carried down to boats on the river]

A word began to travel home with men like Clive. It came from Hindustani. It meant plunder.
By the end of the century it appeared in an English glossary for the first time.
The word was loot.

### CHAPTER TWO: THE DAY A COMPANY BECAME A GOVERNMENT

[SCENE: 1765, a tent at Allahabad, the Mughal emperor seated on a makeshift seat draped in cloth, Clive before him]

Eight years later came the moment that changed everything, and it happened quietly.

In August 1765, at Allahabad, the Mughal emperor Shah Alam the Second signed a document
granting the Company the diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. The diwani was the right to
collect the taxes.

[SCENE: a hand placing a seal on parchment; coins pouring into a Company strongbox]

In exchange, the Company promised the emperor an annual payment of twenty-six lakh rupees.
A rent. For a province that produced many times that.

Think about what that meant. A private company, answerable to shareholders in London, now
had the legal right to tax roughly twenty million people. It could spend that money on
itself, on its army, and on its dividends.

[SCENE: London, a crowd outside East India House on Leadenhall Street, share prices chalked on a board]

In London the share price soared. The Company raised its dividend. Members of Parliament
bought stock. The Company was now not a trading house with a sideline in politics. It was
a state with a sideline in trade.

And a state has to govern. The Company had never learned how.

### CHAPTER THREE: THE FAMINE

[SCENE: Bengal, 1769, dry fields, a dry riverbed, a farmer looking at an empty sky]

In 1769 the rains failed in Bengal. In 1770 they failed again.

Famines had come to Bengal before. What was different this time was who was in charge.
The Company had taken the revenue, but it had left tax collection to the old network of
hereditary collectors, each squeezing what they could. And when the harvest died, the
revenue demand did not.

[SCENE: an empty grain market, a Company official writing in a ledger, villagers beyond the gate]

Grain was hoarded and exported. Relief was late and thin. And in the Company's own
accounts, you can see what it cared about: the revenue receipts for the famine year, and the
letters home that worried about them.

[SCENE: a watercolor of a village, colour draining out of it frame by frame]

Nobody counted the dead. Estimates range enormously, from one million to ten. The figure
most historians accept is that Bengal lost around a third of its people. Warren Hastings,
who took over the province two years later, wrote that figure himself.

A third.

[SCENE: London, the same boardroom, a dividend announcement being read aloud]

That same year, in London, the Company paid its shareholders a dividend of twelve and a half
percent.

### CHAPTER FOUR: THE CRASH

[SCENE: 1772, a London coffee house, a banker fleeing, papers scattered]

It could not last. In June 1772 a London banker named Alexander Fordyce, who had bet heavily
that East India Company shares would fall, lost everything and fled to France. His bank
collapsed. Panic spread through London, then Scotland, then Amsterdam.

[SCENE: a share price chalked on a board, the number falling: 226, then 139]

East India stock went from two hundred and twenty-six in May 1772 to one hundred and
thirty-nine a year later. The Company's warehouses held millions of pounds of unsold tea.
Its revenues from a famine-struck Bengal had collapsed. It had an army to pay.

In January 1773 the Company did what the mightiest corporation on earth had never imagined
doing. It asked the government for a loan.

[SCENE: Parliament, benches, a bill being passed from hand to hand]

Parliament gave it one, well over a million pounds, and attached strings: the Regulating
Act of 1773, the first time the British state took a hand in running India. Then Parliament
passed a second law, to help the Company dump its surplus tea on the American colonies,
tax-free for the Company, taxed for the colonists.

[SCENE: Boston harbour at night, men in disguise tipping chests of tea into black water]

That was the Tea Act. In December 1773, colonists in Boston threw three hundred and forty
chests of Company tea into the harbour.

The bailout of the East India Company helped light the fuse of the American Revolution.

[SCENE: Robert Clive before a parliamentary committee, hands gripping a rail]

That same spring, Parliament called Robert Clive to account for the fortune he had taken at
Plassey. His defence has become famous. He said that after the battle, a great city and a
treasury had lain at his mercy, and that he had taken a fraction of what he could.

"By God, Mr Chairman," he said, "at this moment I stand astonished at my own moderation."

Parliament censured him, and then thanked him for his services. He was dead within two years.

### CLOSE

[SCENE: 1857, smoke over Delhi; then 1858, a Crown seal replacing a Company seal on a door]

The Company staggered on for another eighty years, propped up by Parliament, regulated a
little more each decade, its army growing to around two hundred thousand men, twice the
size of Britain's own.

Then in 1857 its Indian soldiers rose against it. When the rebellion was crushed, the
British state finally took what the Company had built. In 1858 the Crown assumed direct rule
of India. The Company's last shareholders were paid off, and in 1874 it quietly ceased to
exist.

[SCENE: East India House on Leadenhall Street, then the same street today, an office block, people walking past]

Nothing marks the spot. The building was demolished. An insurance company stands there now.

[SCENE: the ledger from the cold open, closed, the candle guttering]

Here is the thing to remember about the East India Company. It did not conquer India with
a plan. It conquered India with a balance sheet. Every step, Plassey, the diwani, the
famine, the bailout, made sense to someone looking at a ledger in London.

That is what makes it frightening. Not the villainy. The accounting.

Next time on Gold and Ruin: the richest man who ever lived, and the day he gave away so much
gold that he crashed the price of gold itself.
